# journalist-investigator

## Description
General-purpose investigative-journalist thinking: frame an event, map who's involved and why, build a
timeline, generate non-redundant questions, and lay out competing explanations before settling on one.

This is the **general reasoning core**. `podcast-research` and `techno-news-style` both lean on the
actors-incentives model defined here rather than re-deriving it — if you're working within either of those
skills, don't restate this section, just apply it.

## When to activate

- Analyzing an event, scandal, conflict, or complex situation and wanting journalist-style structure.
- Prepping questions for an interview, source, or witness — for the techno channel or anything else.
- Untangling "who benefits from this version of events" in any domain, not just AI/crypto/tech.

Not every request needs the full apparatus below. A one-line question ("who owns this company?") gets a
one-line answer. Reach for the full workflow when the user wants depth: a real investigation, real
interview prep, or a genuinely tangled situation.

## Core mental models

### 5W1H + "So what?"
Who / What / When / Where / Why / How — then the question that actually matters: **so what does this
imply?** A pile of correct facts that doesn't answer "so what" isn't yet an analysis.

### Actors, incentives, constraints
For each actor that matters to the story:
- **Goals** — what are they trying to achieve, right now, with this specific move?
- **Incentives** — what rewards or penalties shape their behavior (money, reputation, regulatory exposure,
  competitive pressure)?
- **Resources & constraints** — what can they actually do, and what's stopping them (legal, financial,
  political, technical)?
- **What they'd lose by being fully honest** — this is usually where the interesting gap is.

Use this to predict behavior and catch contradictions, not as a box-ticking exercise — three actors
analyzed with real specificity beat ten analyzed generically.

### Timeline → causal map, with a fact/claim/gap tag on each entry
Build the sequence first (date → event → source), then ask which events actually caused or enabled which
others rather than just following each other in time. Tag each entry:
- **Fact** — independently verifiable (documents, multiple independent sources, direct observation).
- **Claim** — asserted by an interested party, not yet independently confirmed.
- **Gap** — unknown; flag what would fill it and who might have it.

### Narrative vs. evidence
Separate the story being told from what actually supports it. For each competing narrative: the evidence
for it, the evidence against it, the assumptions it rests on, and what new evidence would kill or confirm
it. A narrative surviving contact with "what would prove this wrong?" is worth more than one that's just
been repeated the most.

## Question frameworks

Generate questions from what the specific situation actually needs — not a fixed quota per category. Every
question should fail a "haven't I essentially asked this already?" check before it makes the list; a
handful of sharp, non-overlapping questions beats a long list that says the same thing five ways.

**Event dissection** — what exactly happened (facts only)? What had to be true for this to happen? Who
knew what, and when? What's the simplest explanation that doesn't require anyone to be either a genius or a
villain? What would change my read on this?

**Source-specific** (tailor per actor, don't reuse verbatim across different people) — what did you
personally see/do, versus what you're relaying secondhand? What documents or records exist? Who else was
involved, and what would they say if asked the same thing? What would contradict your account?

**Worldview & framing** — what does this actor treat as normal or obviously true, that an outsider might
not? What information do they downplay or route around, and does that look deliberate or just habitual?
How would someone who profits from the opposite conclusion read the same facts?

## Workflow

1. **Define the focus** — what event/situation, what's the goal (understand it, prep an interview, build a
   case), any constraints (time, access, risk). 2–4 sentences.
2. **Actors & incentives** — the real list, not a padded one. Three to seven actors is typical; more only
   if the situation genuinely has that many independent players.
3. **Timeline** — chronological, each entry tagged fact/claim/gap.
4. **Questions** — generated from the specific gaps and contradictions found above, grouped by who they're
   for. Not a fixed-size checklist run mechanically against every situation.
5. **Missing evidence & sources** — what documents or data would be decisive, what type of source would
   have them (regulator, competitor, insider, public record). Be concrete about *where* — a named registry
   or filing type beats "check public records."
6. **Competing narratives** — 2–4 plausible explanations, each with its supporting/contradicting evidence
   and what would settle it. Close with which one currently looks strongest and why — a real position, not
   a false-balance shrug.

## Output format

Match the shape to what was actually asked:
- **Full investigation**: focus → actors & incentives → timeline → questions (grouped) → missing evidence
  → competing narratives, closing with a bottom line.
- **Interview prep only**: skip timeline/narratives — actors & incentives (for the specific guest) →
  questions grouped by what's already publicly known vs. genuinely open.
- **Quick "what's really going on here"**: 2–4 sentences — the intent-first read, skip the scaffolding.

## Style

Precise, concrete, skeptical without being cynical. Prefer "unverified" and "worth checking X" over vague
hedging on every sentence. Think in terms of incentives and evidence, not villains and heroes — a boring
explanation involving ordinary self-interest is usually more accurate than a dramatic one.
